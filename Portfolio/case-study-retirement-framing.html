01 / My Work: Case Study

Reframing Retirement: Research with Professor Hal Hershfield

Behavioral economics research at UCLA Anderson testing whether changing how people are asked about retirement changes how positively they feel about it, and ultimately how much they might save.

Role Research Assistant
Lab UCLA Anderson Behavioral Lab, until July 2025
Professor Hal Hershfield
Written June 2024

02 / Overview

Overview

As a research assistant in the UCLA Anderson Behavioral Lab, I worked with Professor Hal Hershfield on a study of how people think about retirement. The question was simple to state and hard to answer: if we change how retirement is framed, can we change how people feel about it, and could that eventually help them save more?

I worked on the project from the first literature search through a pilot, a pre-registered 500 person study, and the analysis, which I helped run with Professor Hershfield. I wrote it up in June 2024 as my final paper for Psych 196B. This page walks through what the project found and what I did at each step.

03 / The Problem

A Retirement Savings Crisis

The United States is facing a retirement savings crisis. The share of workers at risk of not having enough funds to maintain their lifestyle in retirement rose from 31% to 53% between 1983 and 2010. Part of the reason is present bias, our tendency to value something now more than the same thing later, which makes retirement feel too far away to save for.

53% Workers at risk of inadequate retirement funds, up from 31% in 1983
~40% Of eligible workers do not use their employer retirement plan

Choice architecture has already shown what is possible. Automatic enrollment helps because most employees simply accept the defaults. But there is still plenty to learn about how people think about saving, and that is where our study came in.

04 / Research Question

Can Framing Change How We Feel About Retirement?

Our research built on the framing effect: the way a choice is presented, as a gain or as a loss, heavily shapes how people think about it and what they decide. Rather than emphasizing gains or losses in a financial sense, we tested whether asking people to picture retirement as a positive phase of life, free from immediate constraints or worries, would change their attitude toward it.

05 / Literature Review

Making Sure the Question Was New

Before any data was collected, I ran the literature review. Using Google Scholar and ResearchRabbit, I searched for prior work on our specific question to make sure no one had already answered it. Four related studies stood out, but none asked how attitude toward retirement shows up in saving behavior.

Narrow framing

Decision style matters

Narrow framers, who focus on immediate choices, were less willing to increase retirement savings than broad framers. This looked at general decision making, not attitude toward retirement.

Social Security

Gain framing delays claiming

People were more likely to delay claiming when later claiming was framed as a gain and anchored at older ages. This studied claiming age, not savings.

Retirement age

Presentation shifts timing

How retirement options are presented can substantially influence when people plan to retire.

Message framing

Gain versus loss messages

Message frame and appeal influence preparedness for retirement and contributions to retirement savings. Closest to our study, but focused on financial gains and losses.

I compiled and summarized these findings for Professor Hershfield, who agreed our question was unique enough to move forward.

06 / Study Design

From Pilot to Pre-Registered Study

  1. Informal exploration. To understand how people think about retirement when simply asked, I gathered opinions from friends and peers, asking them to share a few words on what retirement, and ideal retirement, looked like to them.
  2. Pilot study. After spotting a pattern, we built an open response pilot in Qualtrics and launched it on Amazon's Mechanical Turk with 50 participants. Most people described retirement neutrally, as the end of work, but described ideal retirement as financially stable, relaxing, and time with loved ones.
  3. Main study. We then launched the study on MTurk with 500 participants, 250 asked about their ideal retirement and 250 asked about retirement in general.
  4. Pre-registration. We pre-registered the research design and analysis plan to promote transparency and prevent p-hacking.

Coding the responses

Because the responses were open ended, I encoded each one by attitude (positive, negative, or neutral) and grouped them by theme and by age. I wrote the coding scheme before collecting the main data, and narrowed it to neutral or positive once the pilot showed negative responses were rare. I coded without knowing which condition each response came from, and trained other research assistants to code a sample as a quality check.

07 / Results

The Question Changed the Answer

The main study repeated the pattern we saw in the pilot, and it was large.

93% Positive responses when asked about ideal retirement
38% Positive responses when asked about retirement in general

In the ideal condition the effect held across groups. By age, 92% of participants aged 18 to 35 and 93.7% of those aged 36 to 65 responded positively. By gender, 91.7% of men and 94.6% of women did. The framing effect looks robust across demographics, with small variations by age and gender.

The language differed too. Positive responses commonly mentioned enjoyment, traveling, leisure time, and hobbies, while neutral responses talked about money, security, work, and basic definitions. A likely reason is that when you picture an ideal retirement, it is easy to imagine something good because no time, financial, or other constraints are in the way. Older participants also viewed ideal retirement slightly more positively, possibly because retirement is closer and more tangible for them, so proximity to retirement age is something we will account for in later studies.

08 / What Happened Next

From This Paper to Research with Prudential

The ideal prompt produced overwhelmingly positive responses, so the team planned a more conservative follow-up: asking people for the first thing that comes to mind when they think of retirement, along with questions about their saving habits, to see whether attitude toward retirement shows up in how much people save. I stayed on with Professor Hershfield after June 2024, taking on a range of research tasks including building surveys and collecting data, until I left the lab in July 2025 to move to Chicago.

My work contributed to the larger study Professor Hershfield is working on with Prudential Financial, which grew out of the same question: what do people actually think about when they think about retirement? The project pairs the Anderson research team with Prudential's industry and consumer knowledge, so research topics come from both sides rather than only from the lab. I can't speak to where the specific follow-up studies from this paper ended up, since they continued after I left.

On the Prudential project, I helped run studies in which participants interacted with Future You, an interactive platform the research team used as part of the work, alongside building surveys and collecting data.

Early findings

One early finding, shared publicly by Professor Hershfield, is that near-retirees are thinking about money, but it is not at the top of their list. Human-centered questions come first, like how the move into a new chapter of life will go and how to use their time to stay socially connected. That is a useful wake-up call about the gap between what financial advisors discuss with clients and what clients want to discuss.

Prudential has since described the research as showing that people see retirement as a life transition shaped by freedom, identity, and purpose, rather than only a financial milestone.

Applications

Prudential is putting these insights into practice in two ways. The first is a practice management program for financial professionals, called the New Retirement Conversation, designed to help advisors deepen client relationships and plan around what matters most to each client. The second is an advertising campaign, called Unreal Retirement, that presents retirement as a deeply personal journey rather than a one-size-fits-all outcome.

09 / Other Work

Other Work: Supporting Professor Franklin Shaddy

Alongside my work with Professor Hershfield, I supported Professor Franklin Shaddy's research in the UCLA Anderson Behavioral Lab on two other topics. On both, I wrote the stimuli, checked the survey logic for errors, built the surveys in Qualtrics, launched them on Prolific, collected and analyzed the data, and picked out patterns in the results.

Pain of Paying & The Effect of Bundling

This research asked how bundling changes how painful an add-on feels to buy. When an extra comes packaged with the main purchase, does paying for it hurt less than when it is priced separately? We looked at everyday pairings like a birthday cake with a written message, an iPhone with insurance, theme park tickets with a fast pass, an airplane ticket with a checked bag, and a rental car with gas included.

Example stimulus

Imagine you are ordering a birthday cake for a friend's birthday party. On the website, there are two options, one of which includes a written message on the cake. Which do you prefer?

Option 1: 12 inch cake with no message, $35.00
Option 2: 12 inch cake with custom birthday message, $38.99

Policy: Subsidizing Supply or Demand

When demand is high and supply is low, people tend to back policies that help individuals obtain the product, like coupons, over policies that increase availability. That pushes demand even higher, when expanding supply would be the more sustainable fix. One possible explanation is the identifiable victim effect: people react more to the vivid plight of a single person than to an abstract idea, and helping someone directly feels more vivid than increasing supply.

Example stimulus

A rural town is facing a shortage of baby formula, causing the price of formula to rise. A local store is debating between two solutions to implement. Which do you support more?

Option 1: The store increases stock of baby formula
Option 2: The store hands out coupons to mothers in need to supplement the cost of baby formula

10 / Conclusion

Conclusion

A single change in the question, asking about ideal retirement instead of retirement in general, moved positive responses from 38% to 93%. Working on this project showed me how much of behavioral research is careful groundwork: a literature review that proves the question is new, a coding scheme that holds up, and a pre-registered plan that keeps the results honest.
