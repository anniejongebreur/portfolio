01 / My Work: Case Study

Hotelyr: Boutique Hotel Owner Personas

Research and analytics work for Hotelyr, a guest-experience platform startup for boutique hotels, turning a hotel-owner survey into two customer personas that shaped the company's positioning and pricing.

Role Research & Analytics Intern
Company Hotelyr, Startup Internship
Focus Survey Design, Personas, Market Research

02 / Overview

Overview

Hotelyr is an early-stage startup, founded by David Weber, building a guest-experience platform for independent boutique hotels: the kind of small, personality-driven properties that do not have the budget or staff for the enterprise software larger hotel chains use. As a Research & Analytics Intern, my job was to figure out who Hotelyr's actual customer was, what they cared about, and how that should shape the product and go-to-market plan.

Because boutique hotel owners are a small and fragmented audience, this meant designing original research from scratch rather than pulling from an existing dataset.

03 / Research Methodology

Research Methodology

I built and fielded a survey in Qualtrics aimed at independent boutique hotel owners, reaching out to roughly 40 properties by email and ultimately hearing back from 7, an 8 percent response rate that is typical for cold outreach to busy small-business owners.

I leaned on survey design principles from coursework in consumer research to make the most of a small sample:

  • Question ordering: general questions before specific ones, so early answers do not anchor or bias later responses.
  • Likert scale design: consistent, balanced response scales so answers could be compared across respondents.
  • Randomization: rotating question and option order where relevant to reduce order effects.
  • Social exchange theory: framing the survey so the ask felt proportionate to what a busy owner was willing to give in return, which matters more when your total sample is measured in single digits.

04 / Personas

Two Customer Personas

The clearest pattern in the responses was not geography or price point, it was hotel size. Owners of smaller properties and owners of larger ones described almost opposite priorities, so I split the findings into two personas rather than forcing one average profile.

The Intimate Host

Owns a smaller, more intimate property. Less tech-savvy and cautious about adopting new software. Prioritizes personal, high-touch service over automation and worries a platform like Hotelyr could make the guest experience feel less personal.

The Efficient Operator

Owns a larger boutique property with more rooms and more staff to coordinate. Tech-adept and already using some form of property management software. Prioritizes efficiency and is open to a new platform if it clearly saves time or reduces operational friction.

05 / Competitive Landscape

Competitive Landscape

I mapped the existing guest-experience and hotel-tech tools boutique owners already had access to, Duve, Way, Stay, and TripAround, to understand what Hotelyr needed to do differently rather than duplicate.

Duve

Guest Messaging & Upsell

Strong at automated guest communication and upsell flows, but built for a more standardized, higher-volume property.

Way

All-in-One Ops

A broad operations suite, but a steeper learning curve than a solo boutique owner has time for.

Stay

Guest App Focus

A polished guest-facing app, but light on the owner-side tools smaller properties actually need day to day.

TripAround

Local Recommendations

Good at local concierge-style content, but not a full guest-experience platform on its own.

06 / Recommendations

Recommendations

Based on the two personas above, I recommended four changes to how Hotelyr positioned and sold itself:

  1. Tiered pricing: a lower-commitment tier for the Intimate Host persona and a fuller-featured tier for the Efficient Operator, rather than one-size-fits-all pricing that under-serves one group.
  2. Demo videos: short, concrete walkthroughs aimed at the less tech-savvy persona, who needed to see the product in action before trusting it with their guest relationships.
  3. Referral program: boutique hotel owners trust other boutique hotel owners more than they trust a cold pitch, so word of mouth was worth incentivizing directly.
  4. LinkedIn presence: a more active founder and company presence where boutique hospitality owners and operators actually spend professional time online.

07 / Lean Startup in Practice

Lean Startup Principles in Practice

Working inside an early-stage startup made the lean startup framework, validated learning, and MVP testing feel less like a textbook concept and more like the actual week-to-week rhythm of the company. Hotelyr piloted its MVP in two boutique hotels and a small set of Airbnbs to gather real usage data before committing to a broader build.

That pilot data ended up changing the founder's direction: David pivoted his initial focus from luxury boutique hotels toward friends' Airbnbs, where he could get faster, more honest feedback and iterate more quickly. Watching that pivot happen in real time was one of the clearest lessons of the internship: validated learning only works if you are willing to act on what it tells you, even when it means moving away from your original target customer.

08 / Takeaways

Takeaways

  1. Small samples still need rigorous design. With only 7 responses, question ordering, scale consistency, and framing were not nice-to-haves, they were the difference between usable signal and noise.
  2. Personas earn their keep when they disagree. The most useful output of this research was not a single average customer, it was seeing exactly where the Intimate Host and the Efficient Operator wanted opposite things, which is what actually made the pricing and messaging recommendations concrete.
  3. Startups move on incomplete data, on purpose. Watching a founder pivot off a small pilot rather than waiting for a bigger sample reframed how I think about research timelines: the goal is a decision, not statistical certainty.

09 / Conclusion

Conclusion

This project was my first time owning research end to end inside a real company: designing the instrument, living with a small and imperfect sample, and turning that into personas a founder could actually act on. The tiered pricing and messaging recommendations that came out of it were small in scope, but they were grounded in real conversations with the people Hotelyr was trying to serve, which is what made them worth building around.
