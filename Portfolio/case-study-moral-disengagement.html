01 / My Work: Case Study

Guilt & Self-Serving Justification in Moral Disengagement: Understanding the Psychology of Theft

A full experiment on why people steal even when they know it is wrong, testing how guilt and room for self-serving justification change what people are willing to do.

Course Psych 121, Laboratory in Cognitive Psychology
Professor Patricia Cheng, UCLA
Focus Experimental Design, Qualtrics, Statistical Analysis

02 / Overview

Overview

Behavioral research lives in the gap between how people say they will act and how they actually act once a real decision is in front of them. For Psych 121, Laboratory in Cognitive Psychology, my lab group and I designed and ran a full experiment on moral reasoning and theft, then analyzed the results and wrote them up as a research paper.

03 / The Study

Guilt & Self-Serving Justification in Moral Disengagement

Society treats stealing as unambiguously wrong, yet theft still happens constantly. My lab group, Shreyasi Chaudhary, Tiffany Zheng, Priscilla Yun, and I, set out to ask why: what psychological conditions make someone more or less willing to steal, even when they know it is wrong? I led the results analysis, statistical testing, and discussion of findings, building on the experimental design and background research my teammates developed.

We grounded the study in Albert Bandura's theory of moral disengagement, the idea that people use specific cognitive mechanisms, like diffusing responsibility or dehumanizing a victim, to excuse behavior that conflicts with their own values. We focused on two forces in particular:

  1. Guilt: as guilt increases, people should be less inclined to steal.
  2. Room for self-serving justification: as it becomes easier to craft a story that makes an act seem acceptable, people should be more inclined to steal.
  3. Point of view: people should judge the same act of theft as less acceptable when watching someone else do it than when they imagine doing it themselves.

04 / Study Design

Study Design

We ran a between-subjects experiment in Qualtrics with UCLA undergraduates, randomly assigning each participant to one of four scenarios built around two manipulated variables: guilt (stealing from an individual versus a large corporation) and self-serving justification, or SSJ (a direct act of taking something versus an indirect one, like walking past an unattended booth). Each participant also rated a third-party theft scenario, letting us compare their own hypothetical behavior to how they judged someone else's.

Low Guilt / Low SSJ

Direct, Corporate

Reaching behind an unattended counter to take a movie ticket or a soda from a large chain.

High Guilt / Low SSJ

Direct, Individual

Taking a ticket or drink left unattended by another person nearby.

Low Guilt / High SSJ

Indirect, Corporate

Movie hopping or refilling a water cup with soda at a large chain.

High Guilt / High SSJ

Indirect, Individual

Taking an item someone dropped, where they could plausibly come back for it.

05 / Key Findings

Key Findings

Both manipulated variables moved behavior in the predicted direction, and both effects were statistically significant.

-41% Likelihood of theft, low guilt vs. high guilt
+122% Likelihood of theft, low vs. high self-serving justification
+193% Comfort stealing from a corporation vs. an individual

Guilt worked as an emotional brake: on a 1 to 10 likelihood scale, participants in high guilt conditions reported a mean likelihood of theft of 1.87 versus 3.16 in low guilt conditions (p < .001), and they rated the harm of that same action higher too. Self-serving justification worked in the opposite direction, more room to construct a story that made the act feel acceptable meant a much higher stated likelihood of stealing (1.60 versus 3.55, p < .001). Participants were also far more comfortable stealing from a large corporation than from an individual (1.51 versus 4.42 on a 10-point comfort scale, p < .001), which lines up with Bandura's dehumanization of victim mechanism: it is easier to justify harming an abstract corporation than a specific person.

The most interesting result was the one that did not hold. We expected people to judge a third party's theft more harshly than their own, since self-serving narratives should not extend to other people. Instead, participants rated a third party's theft as more acceptable than their own in almost every condition. That result pointed toward something more interesting than our original hypothesis: people may extend empathy, and the same justifying narratives, to others more readily than the fundamental attribution error would predict.

06 / Limitations

Limitations

  • A small convenience sample of UCLA undergraduates limits how far these results generalize to other populations.
  • Open-ended justification responses had to be hand-coded into Bandura's mechanisms for analysis, which introduces some subjectivity.
  • All scenarios were hypothetical. Stated intent to steal is a useful proxy for behavior, but it is not the same as observing real theft decisions.

07 / Conclusion

Conclusion

Guilt and room for self-serving justification both moved behavior the way we predicted, and the one hypothesis that failed, that people would judge a stranger's theft more harshly than their own, turned out to be the most interesting result. Running this study end to end, from hypothesis through a finding that overturned one of our own assumptions, taught me that the surprising result is usually where the real insight is.
